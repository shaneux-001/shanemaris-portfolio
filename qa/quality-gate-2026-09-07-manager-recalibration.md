# 3. P0 — Add People-Leadership Evidence

## Problem

The portfolio currently proves leadership of systems, tooling, governance, migration, adoption, budgets, and organizational constraints more strongly than leadership of individual designers and design teams.

For Design Manager / Senior Design Manager roles, this is the largest remaining gap.

The portfolio should answer:

- How do you coach designers?
- How do you raise the quality bar?
- How do you delegate?
- How do you handle disagreement?
- How do you protect a team?
- How do you develop people into larger roles?
- How do you manage conflicting priorities?
- How do you handle underperformance or stalled work?
- How do you create clarity when authority is limited?
- What changed in another person’s capability because of your leadership?

## Required change

Surface **real people-leadership moments** throughout the site.

Do not fabricate a separate management case study unless there is enough genuine material to support one.

### Potential surfaces

- About page
- Case-study executive summaries
- Case-study narrative moments
- Resume
- Leadership principles / beliefs
- Project outcome sections

### Acceptance criteria

- [ ] At least 3 concrete people-leadership examples appear across the portfolio.
- [ ] At least one example shows coaching or developing another designer.
- [ ] At least one example shows delegation or transferring ownership.
- [ ] At least one example shows conflict, disagreement, prioritization, or difficult alignment.
- [ ] Team outcomes are not written as solely individual accomplishments.
- [ ] Management examples identify what the candidate actually did.
- [ ] Examples show judgment, not generic “collaboration.”
- [ ] People-leadership language remains grounded in real events.
- [ ] Any missing evidence is marked `NEEDS_OWNER_INPUT` rather than generated.

# 7. P1 — Add Case-Study Scanability Without Executive Theater

## Problem

The long-form case studies are credible and human, but reviewers must read too far before understanding the important decision and outcome.

For Manager / Senior Manager hiring, preserve the narrative while adding a fast orientation layer.

## Required change

Add a compact intro block to each flagship case study.

### Recommended structure

**MY ROLE**  
One concise line.

**THE PROBLEM**  
One sharp sentence.

**SCALE**  
2–3 useful metrics.

**WHAT CHANGED**  
2–3 concise outcomes.

**THE STORY ↓**

Do not build a seven-card corporate dashboard.

### Acceptance criteria

- [ ] Heart Design System includes a compact orientation block.
- [ ] Figma Enterprise Migration includes a compact orientation block.
- [ ] Proof Before Progress includes a compact orientation block.
- [ ] Reader can understand role/problem/scale/outcome in under ~45–60 seconds.
- [ ] Existing narrative remains intact.
- [ ] Metrics are specific and factual.
- [ ] Role ownership is clear.
- [ ] Summary does not become visually heavier than the case study itself.
- [ ] No generic `Problem → Research → Personas → Wireframes → Solution` structure unless factually appropriate.

# 8. P1 — Preserve the Human Narrative

## Why this matters

The current portfolio’s strongest writing signal is that it admits:

- incomplete work
- organizational ambiguity
- failed or paused initiatives
- decisions that were right only for a specific moment
- unresolved issues
- imperfect understanding
- changing constraints

That creates trust.

## Required change

Do not over-edit the portfolio into corporate case-study language.

### Preferred anti-AI test

Ask:

> **Could this sentence have been written by any designer about any project?**

If yes, cut or sharpen it.

If the sentence contains a real observation, decision, consequence, opinion, or specific detail, preserve it.

### Acceptance criteria

- [ ] Candid admissions remain.
- [ ] Failed and paused work remains.
- [ ] Unresolved outcomes remain where useful.
- [ ] Decisions retain contextual nuance.
- [ ] Corporate marketing language is not introduced.
- [ ] Writing still sounds like a person with firsthand experience.
- [ ] No generic “hero’s journey” rewrite.

# 9. P1 — Reduce Repetitive Cinematic Copy

## Problem

Short dramatic conclusions can be effective, but repetition creates a recognizable writing formula.

Examples of the pattern:

> It wasn’t official. But it was real.

> That was enough. For a while, that had to be.

## Required change

Reduce repeated short-sentence dramatic endings by roughly 25–30%.

Do not remove all personality.

### Flag for review

- repeated one-line dramatic paragraphs
- repeated “It wasn’t X. It was Y.”
- repeated “That was enough.”
- excessive em dashes
- “not just X, but Y”
- “more than X”
- “revolutionizing”
- “transformative”
- “seamless”
- “empowering”
- “delve”
- “unlock”
- “reimagine”
- unsupported “innovative”
- generic “human-centered” claims

### Acceptance criteria

- [ ] Dramatic endings are occasional rather than structural.
- [ ] Em dashes are not the dominant rhythm device.
- [ ] No obvious LLM buzzword clusters.
- [ ] Concrete nouns and decisions dominate over adjectives.
- [ ] Copy remains conversational and human.
- [ ] The strongest distinctive lines are preserved.

# 25. Final Product Principle

The site should not attempt to prove:

> **“I am already a VP.”**

It should convincingly demonstrate:

> **“I am a strong design leader who improves teams, systems, and the environment around product design—and I have unusually deep experience making that infrastructure work in the real world.”**

That is the strongest current **Manager / Senior Manager** story, while leaving credible room for Director-level opportunities where scope and company size align.
